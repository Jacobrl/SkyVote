# Moon phases: fact cards

Facts in our own words, with sources from `sources.md`. Directions like
"right" and "left" describe the view from the Northern Hemisphere unless
a card says otherwise.

---

## MP-01: Moonlight is sunlight

The Moon makes no light of its own. What we call moonlight is sunlight
bouncing off the Moon's surface.

- Sources: nasa, lpi, khan

## MP-02: Half the Moon is always lit

Like Earth, the Moon has a day side and a night side. The Sun always
lights up half of it. What changes is how much of that lit half we can
see from Earth.

- Sources: nasa, earthsky, lpi, khan, planetary

## MP-03: Why phases happen

As the Moon travels around Earth, the angle between the Sun, Earth and
Moon changes. Sometimes we face mostly the Moon's day side, sometimes
mostly its night side, and usually a mix. The Moon itself never changes
shape.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, planetary, lpi, earthsky, khan

## MP-04: We always see the same side

The Moon spins once on its axis in the same time it takes to orbit
Earth once. Because of this, the same side, called the near side,
always faces us. This is called tidal locking, or synchronous rotation.

- Sources: nasa, lpi, khan, planetary

## MP-05: There is no "dark side" of the Moon

The far side of the Moon gets sunlight just like the near side does. It
is only "hidden" from Earth, not dark. Spacecraft have mapped it.

- Sources: lpi, nasa

## MP-06: The eight phases, in order

New Moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full Moon,
waning gibbous, last quarter, waning crescent. Then the cycle starts
again. "Last quarter" and "third quarter" are two names for the same
phase.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, khan, planetary, moongiant

## MP-07: Main phases and in-between phases

New Moon, first quarter, full Moon and last quarter are the four main
phases. They happen at an exact moment. The other four (the crescents
and gibbous phases) are the stretches of days between them.

- Sources: moongiant, usno
- Note: The "exact moment" part comes from the USNO data, which gives a
  single time for each main phase only.

## MP-08: How long the cycle takes

One full cycle of phases, from one new Moon to the next, takes about
29.5 days. This is called the synodic month. MoonGiant gives it more
exactly as 29.53 days.

- Sources: nasa, lpi, khan, moongiant
- Note: The Planetary Society rounds it to "about 30 days".

## MP-09: Why the phase cycle is longer than one orbit

The Moon goes once around Earth, measured against the stars, in about
27.3 days (27 days, 7 hours, 43 minutes). This is the sidereal month.
But Earth is also moving around the Sun, so the Moon has to travel a
bit farther to line up with the Sun the same way again. That is why the
phases take about 29.5 days.

- Sources: nasa, lpi, khan

## MP-10: What the phase words mean

"Waxing" means growing: the lit part gets bigger each night. "Waning"
means shrinking. "Crescent" is a thin curved sliver. "Gibbous" comes
from a Latin word for humpbacked, and means more than half lit but not
full.

- Sources: khan, moongiant, catalina, planetary, lpi

## MP-11: Why a "half" Moon is called a quarter

At first and last quarter, the Moon looks half lit. But we only ever see
one half of the Moon, so half of that half is a quarter of the whole
Moon. The name also fits because these phases come a quarter and three
quarters of the way through the cycle.

- Sources: nasa, planetary, khan

## MP-12: Why crescents look curved

The Moon is a ball. When only a thin edge of its lit side faces us, that
edge follows the Moon's round outline, so it looks like a curve.

- Sources: planetary

## MP-13: Northern and Southern Hemisphere views

From the Northern Hemisphere, a waxing Moon lights up from the right
side toward the left, and a waning Moon darkens from the right. From the
Southern Hemisphere it is the other way around. The phases still happen
at the same moment everywhere on Earth.

- Sources: lpi

## MP-14: Earth has phases too

If you stood on the Moon, Earth would show phases, in the opposite order.
When we see a new Moon, Earth would look full from the Moon.

- Sources: britannica

## MP-15: Earthshine

During crescent phases you can sometimes see the dark part of the Moon
glowing faintly. That is sunlight bouncing off Earth onto the Moon, and
it is called earthshine.

- Sources: nasa, khan

## MP-20: New Moon

The Moon is roughly between Earth and the Sun, so its night side faces
us and we can barely see it. It sits in the same part of the sky as the
Sun, rising and setting with it.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, lpi, planetary, khan
- Note: NASA points out the Moon usually passes a little above or below
  the Sun, not directly in front of it, because its orbit is tilted.
  Britannica, LPI and the Planetary Society simply say "between".

## MP-21: Waxing crescent

A thin, growing sliver, lit on the right. About a quarter of the Moon's
face is lit. It rises in the morning after the Sun and sets in the
evening after the Sun. If you see a thin crescent in the west after
sunset, it is setting, not rising.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, earthsky, lpi

## MP-22: First quarter

Seen from Earth, the Moon and Sun form a right angle. Half of the face
we see is lit. It rises around midday, sets around the middle of the
night, and is high in the evening sky, which makes it easy to watch.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, khan, planetary

## MP-23: Waxing gibbous

More than half lit but not yet full, about three-quarters. It comes
between one and two weeks after the new Moon. It rises in the afternoon,
is high in the east at sunset and sets after midnight, before sunrise.
It is often easy to spot in the daytime.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, catalina, moongiant

## MP-24: Full Moon

Earth is roughly between the Sun and the Moon, so the Moon's whole day
side faces us. It rises around sunset and sets around sunrise, and it
looks full for a couple of days.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, lpi, planetary, khan

## MP-25: Waning gibbous

After the full Moon, the lit part starts to shrink, but most of the face
is still lit (about three-quarters). It rises at night after sunset and
sets in the morning after sunrise.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, planetary

## MP-26: Last quarter (third quarter)

The Moon and Sun form a right angle again. Half the face is lit, but it
is the opposite half from the first quarter. It rises around the middle
of the night and sets around midday.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, planetary, khan

## MP-27: Waning crescent

A thin, shrinking sliver, on the opposite side from the waxing crescent.
About a quarter of the face is lit. It rises in the very early morning,
before the Sun, and sets in the afternoon. Then the cycle returns to
new Moon.

- Sources: britannica, nasa, planetary

## MP-30: The Moon rises later each day

The Moon moves about 12 to 13 degrees east against the stars each day as
it orbits. Earth has to turn a little longer to catch up, so the Moon
rises about 50 minutes later each day, on average.

- Sources: earthsky, nasa

## MP-31: Rising in the east, setting in the west

Like the Sun and stars, the Moon rises in the east and sets in the west
every day. That is caused by Earth spinning toward the east.

- Sources: earthsky

## MP-32: Seeing the Moon in daytime

The Moon is often up during the day. When and where you can see it
depends on its phase, the time of year and where you are. Gibbous Moons
are easy to spot in daylight because so much of the face is lit and the
Moon is far from the Sun's glare.

- Sources: nasa, catalina

## MP-33: Why eclipses don't happen every month

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees compared to Earth's orbit
around the Sun. So at most new and full Moons, the Moon passes a little
above or below the line between Earth and the Sun. Only when they line
up do we get a solar eclipse (at new Moon) or a lunar eclipse (at full
Moon).

- Sources: lpi, planetary, nasa
- Note: The Planetary Society says solar eclipses happen two to five
  times a year. LPI says lunar eclipses happen two to four times a year.
  Eclipses are out of scope for launch, so check these before using them
  on the site.

## MP-34: Libration: the Moon's wobble

Over a month, the Moon seems to rock and nod a little, so from Earth we
can see slightly more than half of its surface over time. This is called
libration. It happens because the Moon's orbit is an oval, its orbit is
tilted, and Earth is tilted.

- Sources: nasa

## MP-35: How far away the Moon is

The Moon is on average about 384,400 km (238,855 miles) from Earth, about
the width of 30 Earths.

- Sources: nasa

## MP-36: The Moon, tides and Earth's tilt

The Moon's gravity drives the tides. It also helps keep Earth's tilt
steady over long periods, which keeps our seasons and weather more
stable.

- Sources: khan
- Note: Single source, and not about phases. Check before using on the
  site.
